Hi, I’m Vamshi Kanderao. First, what Déjà Vu is not: it’s not another chatbot. It’s an infer-then-confirm, user-owned, bounded memory layer for everyday personal agents. It’s a memory layer that any personal agent can add, Muse, ChatGPT, Claude, or your own, so it remembers you the way a good assistant would. It learns short cards from your chats, uses only the ones that fit each request, checks with you once when a guess really matters, and lets short-term things go on their own date. So the idea is one line: inferred versus confirmed. The agent keeps learning from chats and keeps using everything it learned, exactly like today: confirming is never a gate. What changes is that every card is marked as a guess or as confirmed, and when a guess is about to shape something that matters, like a booking, it asks once, in one line, and the answer still comes first. What you confirm wins over what it guessed. That gives you trust, and it keeps memory small and cheap. Let me show you.

Here’s the flow. Your chats go in, years of them. In the background, Muse Spark writes short cards, and Muse Wonder, a second model, grades each one against your own words before it’s saved. Each card is marked as a guess or as confirmed. You confirm with one swipe. Then when you ask something, Muse Spark answers using only the few cards that matter for that question. Three properties make this work: it’s bounded, so cost is predictable; it’s scoped, so a dinner question never sees your work cards; and it’s yours, exportable as a single file.

Here’s the engine for one message. Step one: Muse Spark picks the few cards this request needs, which ones are key, and how high the stakes are. One call over a bounded index, about a few hundred tokens of cards instead of years of history. Step two is plain code, the don’t-nag rule: it only asks when a key card is a guess, the stakes are real, and nothing was asked in this chat. Usually the answer is no. Step three: the answer, right away. If it’s a booking or an order, Muse Spark calls a draft tool, so you get a real draft built from your memory, and the check line rides above it. Step four is you: tap still true, tap not anymore, or just keep chatting, which counts as a quiet yes. Step five happens after the answer: one model writes new cards, code rules and a second model, Muse Wonder, check them, and only then do they enter your memory. Every step is visible under the info button, and any agent can use the same layer over three HTTP calls.

08 / 15

The risk with asking is nagging. So there’s a strict rule, and it’s plain code, not a model guess. The agent only asks when all four are true: the card actually changes this answer, it’s unsure about it, the stakes are real, and it hasn’t asked recently. Most conversations get no question at all. It’s plain code, not a model guess, so it’s predictable. And when you just keep chatting after a check, that counts as a quiet yes; two of them confirm the card. So the longer you use it, the less it asks. Insights shows it as a number: checks per hundred answers, and why it stayed quiet the rest of the time.

09 / 15

Not everything you tell an agent is forever. Maria said in July: no takeout for the next three months, she’s saving for her son’s robotics camp. A normal memory would keep that forever and keep steering her away from takeout. Déjà Vu writes it as a short-term card with an end date, counted from the day she said it. Until then it’s used like any other card. On the end date, chat asks once: keep it going for a month, three months, keep it for good, change it, or let it end. After the date passes, it simply stops being used. Long-term facts work exactly as before.

10 / 15

Before anything enters memory, it passes a gate. One model writes cards from your chats, and every card has to cite the messages it came from. Then plain code rules check it for free: short, backed by something you actually wrote, no labels about who you are, nothing about the agent itself, and anything about health or money is marked private. Then a second model, Muse Wonder, grades each card against those messages: keep, mark private, or reject because it isn’t backed by the chat, it’s a label, a one-off, or already known. The writer never approves its own work. What it doesn’t reject is the small stuff: a usual drink, a favorite artist, what your mom likes. Those are what make help feel personal. If the grader is ever down, learning falls back to the rules alone and never blocks. Insights shows what was kept and what wasn’t, and why.

Trust comes from receipts. Every card links back to the exact conversation it came from. Sensitive things like health or money are marked private: used carefully, checked once when they shape something that matters, and yours to remove any time. Confirming is never a gate in front of memory. And the whole memory is a portable file you can take to any agent. Does it work? We tested three ways an agent can remember you, on fourteen people: two real chat histories and twelve synthetic people, 312 checks judged by Muse Spark, each counted once. A memory summary got 72.4 percent right, re-reading the full history 75, and Déjà Vu memory 80.4. When life changed in ways that never came up in chat, Déjà Vu got 80.6 percent; the others, 49 and 56. It sends about 380 tokens per answer, by the API’s own count, against about 600 for a summary and about 7,000 for the full history. Déjà Vu is best or tied for 12 of the 14 people. To be honest about the method: ten of the synthetic people were written after the engine was built, and the builder was tuned with two of their builds in view, so they are not a clean held-out set.

13 / 15

Every Meta model here has one job, picked for that job. Muse Spark 1.3 writes the answers, picks the cards each request needs, and makes the drafts, because it’s agent-ready: tool calls come back as structured drafts, and it’s fast at low reasoning. Muse Wonder is the grader: a different, stronger model checks every card Muse Spark writes, so the model that writes a card never approves it. Cards are fourteen words at most, so a batch costs under a cent. Muse Voice Transcribe lets you answer a check by voice, hands-free, ready for glasses. Next: the background memory work and the locked sensitive cards move to Meta’s open weights, Muse Spark Open or Glimmer, so the most personal processing runs on weights you control. After that, SAM 3.1 and vision turn a photo of a ticket or receipt into cards, and Muse Wonder also becomes the independent judge for our eval.

14 / 15

All of this comes back to one design rule: don’t change what works. People already like chatting with their agent, so memory rides along instead of taking over. It’s lightweight: the chat stays the chat, with one line above an answer, an info button under it, and a section in the menu. It never blocks: the answer always comes first, checks ride along, learning happens after, and a redo only happens when you ask. And it’s optional: any agent can plug it in over three calls, and anyone can turn it off with one switch.
